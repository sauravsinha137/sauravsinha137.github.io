Designing Uber: A Deep Walkthrough

A long-form writeup of the classic "design a ride-sharing service" interview problem, inspired by the Hello Interview breakdown, with my own diagrams, numbers, and commentary layered on top. Diagrams are in Mermaid — render in any Mermaid-aware viewer (GitHub, Obsidian, VS Code).

Uber looks deceptively simple from the outside: two apps, one button, a car shows up. Underneath, it's one of the hardest classes of system to build well, because it combines three problems that are individually hard:

  1. A geospatial firehose — millions of GPS pings per second that must be queryable in real time
  2. A distributed mutual-exclusion problem — a driver is a physical resource that cannot be double-booked
  3. A human-in-the-loop workflow — humans take seconds to respond, ignore their phones, and drive through tunnels

Most of the interesting design decisions fall out of these three tensions. Let's build it up.

1. Scoping the Problem

Functional requirements (the top 4)

  1. Riders enter a pickup + destination and get a fare estimate
  2. Riders can request a ride against that estimate
  3. The system matches the rider with a nearby, available driver
  4. Drivers can accept/decline and navigate to pickup/drop-off

Deliberately below the line: ratings, scheduled rides, ride tiers (X/XL/Comfort), payments. In a 45-minute interview, scoping is the skill — the interviewer wants to see you pick the 3–4 requirements that expose the hard parts and park the rest explicitly.

Non-functional requirements — where the real design lives

NFR Why it's the hard part
Low-latency matching (match or fail < 1 min) Matching is a multi-step, human-gated workflow. Every second of driver hesitation eats your budget.
Strong consistency in matching A driver is a physical resource. Double-assigning one isn't an eventual-consistency inconvenience — it's two riders standing on curbs and one of them getting stood up.
Burst throughput (~100k requests from one geo cell) Demand is violently non-uniform: a concert ends, a metro line dies, it starts raining. Your quiet-Tuesday architecture must survive New Year's Eve.

Notice the shape: the FRs give you the boxes, the NFRs give you the deep dives. This is a general truth of system design interviews.

Two modelling notes worth calling out:

  • Fare as a separate entity is a quote pattern. The estimate is a server-signed offer with its own lifecycle (created → maybe accepted → expired). Splitting it out means the client can never tamper with the price — the ride request only carries a fareId, and the server looks up the number it committed to. It also gives you a natural place for quote expiry ("this fare is valid for 5 minutes").
  • Location is not really a table. It's drawn as one here, but the whole first deep dive is about why it must not live in your primary database. Hold that thought.

3. API Surface

POST  /fare                → Fare        { pickupLocation, destination }
POST  /rides               → Ride        { fareId }
POST  /drivers/location    → 200/4xx     { lat, long }
PATCH /rides/:rideId       → Ride        { action: "accept" | "deny" }

The API is small — four endpoints cover everything above. What matters more than the endpoints is the trust boundary discipline:

  • riderId / driverId come from the session or JWT, never the body. A body-supplied ID is an impersonation vector.
  • Timestamps are server-generated. Client clocks lie, both accidentally and maliciously.
  • The fare amount is looked up from the DB via fareId. If the client could send fare: 0.01, someone would.

This shows up constantly in interview feedback: candidates who pass userId in the request body are signalling they've never thought about where the trust boundary sits. The same instinct applies at TrueCredits-style fintech scale — anything that touches money must be re-derived server-side.

Note there's no "match me a driver" endpoint. Matching is a backend workflow triggered by POST /rides, not something the client orchestrates. The client's job ends at expressing intent.

Five services, each with one job:

Service Responsibility Why it's separate
API Gateway Auth, rate limiting, routing Cross-cutting concerns don't belong in business services
Ride Service Fare quotes + ride state machine Owns the source of truth for a ride's lifecycle
Location Service Absorb the GPS firehose Its write profile (2M/s, tiny payloads, overwrite semantics) is nothing like the ride path's — different scaling story entirely
Ride Matching Service Find and offer drivers The workflow brain; scales with demand, not with driver count
Notification Service APNs/FCM fan-out Push delivery is its own operational domain (token management, retries, platform quirks)

The Location/Matching split is the one candidates most often miss. Location ingestion is a write-heavy, loss-tolerant, latest-value-wins problem. Matching is a read-heavy, correctness-critical problem. Coupling them means scaling both for the worst case of either.

That innocent-looking alt box — "or times out" — is deep dives 3 and 5. It's where most of the system's real complexity hides.

5. Deep Dive 1 — The GPS Firehose

The math that kills the naive design: 10M active drivers × 1 ping / 5s = 2M writes/sec. On DynamoDB on-demand pricing that's roughly $200k+/day just to record positions. On Postgres it's a wall of WAL and vacuum pressure. And it buys you nothing, because the reads are broken too: proximity search on lat/long columns with B-tree indexes degenerates to scans — B-trees order one dimension at a time, and "near (x, y)" is irreducibly two-dimensional.

Three escalating answers:

❌ Naive: write every ping to the primary DB

Dead on arrival at this scale. Worth stating in an interview only to establish the baseline you're escaping.

🟡 Better: batch writes + a real geospatial index

Buffer pings, flush in batches, and use a proper spatial structure — a quad-tree (recursively split the plane into quadrants; dense areas subdivide deeper) or Postgres with PostGIS (R-tree-backed GiST indexes). Batching slashes write ops; the spatial index makes radius queries logarithmic-ish.

The flaw is the batching window itself: your view of driver positions is always one flush interval stale. A driver doing 60 km/h moves ~85m in 5 seconds. In a dense city grid, stale data means offering rides to drivers who just turned the wrong way.

✅ Best: Redis GEO — treat location as ephemeral, in-memory state

The reframe that unlocks this: driver location isn't data you store; it's state you overwrite. You never need position history for matching — only the latest fix. That's exactly what an in-memory store is for.

Redis's GEO commands are built on a sorted set. GEOADD interleaves the bits of latitude and longitude into a 52-bit geohash used as the member's score. Interleaving means nearby points get numerically close scores, so a radius query (GEOSEARCH) becomes a handful of range scans over the sorted set plus a precise distance filter — microseconds, in memory.

# every ping:
GEOADD drivers:blr 77.5946 12.9716 driver:8842      # overwrite = free "compaction"
ZADD   drivers:blr:seen 1720877700 driver:8842       # companion freshness set

# matching:
GEOSEARCH drivers:blr FROMLONLAT 77.60 12.97 BYRADIUS 2 km ASC COUNT 20

# liveness sweep (every ~10s):
ZRANGEBYSCORE drivers:blr:seen 0 <now - 30s>          # → ZREM + GEO ZREM

Because each GEOADD replaces the previous entry, there is no unbounded growth and no batching delay — the store is the current world state. Stale drivers (app killed, tunnel, dead battery) are reaped by the companion timestamp set.

"But Redis isn't durable!" — correct, and here it doesn't matter, which is the elegant part. If the whole cluster loses its memory, drivers repopulate it within one ping cycle (~5s). The data's natural refresh rate exceeds any realistic recovery time. Add RDB/AOF persistence and Sentinel failover as belt-and-braces, but the architecture is safe even without them. This is a great interview moment: recognizing which data actually needs durability, instead of reflexively demanding it everywhere.

6. Deep Dive 2 — Shrinking the Firehose at the Source

Even with Redis absorbing 2M writes/sec comfortably, most of those pings are informationally worthless. A driver parked at a chai stall for 20 minutes sends 240 identical updates.

The fix lives on the client: adaptive ping intervals. The driver app already has an accelerometer, GPS speed, and knowledge of its own state — use them:

Context Interval Rationale
Stationary, no pending offer 30–60s Position isn't changing; a heartbeat suffices
Slow / stop-and-go traffic 10–15s Low positional drift per interval
Highway speed or frequent turns 2–3s 25 m/s means 5s gaps = 125m errors
Offer pending / en route to pickup max frequency Rider is watching the car icon

Realistic effect: an order-of-magnitude reduction in aggregate pings, since the fleet skews heavily toward idle-and-slow at any moment.

The transferable lesson — and interviewers explicitly look for it — is don't draw the client as a dumb box. The same move shows up everywhere: file-upload services push chunking and compression to the client; sync engines push diffing to the client. The client is a distributed fleet of computers you already pay nothing to run.

7. Deep Dive 3 — Never Double-Book a Driver

The consistency NFR, concretely: at any instant, one driver ↔ at most one outstanding offer, and the offer holds for a bounded window (10s) before the system moves on. Structurally identical to Ticketmaster's seat hold: reserve a contended resource for a short window, release automatically if unclaimed.

❌ App-level locks (in-process map + timer)

Multiple Matching Service instances each keeping their own lock table means no shared truth: two instances can offer the same driver simultaneously (race), and a crashed instance takes its locks and timers to the grave, leaving drivers frozen in a phantom "offered" state. In-process state for cross-instance coordination is the root error.

🟡 Status column in the DB

Move the lock into the database and let transactions arbitrate:

UPDATE drivers SET status = 'outstanding_request'
WHERE driver_id = ? AND status = 'available';
-- 1 row updated → you hold the driver. 0 rows → someone beat you.

The compare-and-swap in the WHERE clause fixes coordination cleanly. What's still broken is the release path: expiring the hold after 10s relies on an in-memory timer (dies with the process) or a sweep cron (adds lag and moving parts). Locks acquired reliably but released unreliably are how you end up with a fleet of permanently "busy" drivers at 2 a.m.

✅ Redis lock with TTL — let the datastore own the deadline

SET driver_lock:8842 ride:xyz NX PX 10000

NX gives atomic acquire-if-absent; PX 10000 attaches a 10-second self-destruct. The genius is that expiry is the datastore's job, not your process's. Every failure mode collapses into the same safe outcome:

stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Available
    Available --> Locked: SET NX PX 10000 succeeds
    Locked --> Accepted: driver taps accept<br/>(DB update + DEL lock)
    Locked --> Available: driver declines (DEL)
    Locked --> Available: 10s TTL expires<br/>(driver asleep, app dead,<br/>matcher crashed — same result)
    Accepted --> [*]

Matcher crashes mid-offer? TTL expires, driver frees. Driver drops phone in the passenger seat? TTL expires. Network partition? TTL expires. You've replaced three failure-handling code paths with zero.

The dependency on Redis availability is real but proportionate: locks live 10 seconds. Even a total Redis loss self-heals within one offer window — the same ephemerality argument as the GEO store. (For those who've read about Redlock debates: single-instance + Sentinel is defensible here precisely because the correctness window is so short and the downside is a re-offered ride, not corrupted money.)

8. Deep Dive 4 — Surviving the Concert Letting Out

100k ride requests from one stadium in two minutes. Two distinct failure modes to defend against: overload (requests arrive faster than matchers can process) and loss (a matcher instance dies holding in-flight requests).

Synchronous processing fails both: autoscaling reacts in minutes, spikes happen in seconds; and a crashed instance's in-flight requests simply vanish — a rider staring at a spinner for a match that will never come.

✅ Kafka between Ride Service and Matching Service

flowchart LR
    RS[Ride Service] -->|produce ride request| K
    subgraph K [Kafka — partitioned by geo region]
        P1[blr-south]
        P2[blr-north]
        P3[mum-central]
    end
    P1 --> M1[Matcher 1]
    P2 --> M2[Matcher 2]
    P3 --> M3[Matcher 3]
    AS[Autoscaler] -.->|consumer lag high → add matchers| M3

The queue buys three things at once:

  1. Elasticity — the queue absorbs the spike; matchers drain at their own pace and scale on consumer lag, a far better autoscaling signal than CPU.
  2. Durability — the make-or-break detail: commit the consumer offset only after the match completes. A matcher that dies mid-match never committed, so Kafka redelivers to the next consumer in the group. Crash-safety falls out of the offset protocol for free. (Same pattern as SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED job queues — claim, process, then ack — just with Kafka's offset as the ack.)
  3. Locality — partitioning by geo region means Bengaluru's surge never queues behind Mumbai's, and each matcher holds a warm view of one region's drivers.

Two honest caveats: it's more infrastructure to run (managed Kafka — MSK/Confluent — is the sane default), and strict FIFO within a partition invites head-of-line blocking behind one slow match. If that bites, layer priority semantics on top (separate high/low-priority topics, or re-enqueue slow matches).

9. Deep Dive 5 — The Driver Who Never Answers

Deep dive 3 freed the driver when an offer times out. But who advances the ride to the next candidate? A sleep(10) in the matcher is a bug: the process restarts and the ride is orphaned forever. This is the human-in-the-loop problem in its purest form — you need timeouts and retries that outlive the process that scheduled them.

🟡 Delay queue

On offering driver N, also enqueue a message with a 10s delivery delay: "check ride X." When it fires, if the ride is still unassigned, offer driver N+1 and schedule the next check. The timer now survives crashes because it lives in the queue.

Workable, but the cancellation semantics get fiddly: a driver who accepts at second 9.8 races the already-scheduled check, so the check handler must re-verify state atomically (against the DB status or the Redis lock) before acting. You end up hand-assembling a state machine out of queue messages and conditional checks — every edge case is yours to find.

✅ Durable execution (Temporal / Step Functions)

The cleaner answer: stop assembling the state machine and write it as code in a framework that persists execution state:

workflow matchRide(rideId):
    for driver in rankedCandidates(rideId):
        offer(driver)                        # activity
        result = await accept OR timer(10s)  # durable — survives crashes
        if result == accepted:
            return assign(driver)
        # decline or timeout → next candidate
    return noDriversAvailable(rideId)

Every await is checkpointed. Kill the worker at any line; another worker resumes at exactly that line with all state intact. Timeouts, retries, and the accept-vs-timer race are framework primitives instead of hand-rolled queue choreography.

The historical footnote makes this answer land especially well: Uber built Cadence — Temporal's direct ancestor — precisely because ride matching is this problem. The canonical company's answer to the canonical question was "build a durable execution engine."

Cost: a new orchestration system to operate and a real learning curve (deterministic workflow code, versioning). For a flow where every dropped request is lost revenue and a stranded human, the trade is usually right.

10. Deep Dive 6 — Scaling Out: Geography Is the Shard Key

Vertical scaling gets one sentence: it's a ceiling, a cost curve, and a single point of failure — mention it only to dismiss it.

The right lens: a ride in Bengaluru never needs to know about a driver in Mumbai. Ride-sharing is embarrassingly partitionable by geography, so shard everything — services, Kafka topics, Redis GEO stores, databases — by region, and add read replicas within each shard for read-heavy paths.

flowchart TB
    subgraph BLR [Bengaluru shard]
        A1[Services] --> A2[(Redis GEO)] & A3[(DB + replicas)]
    end
    subgraph MUM [Mumbai shard]
        B1[Services] --> B2[(Redis GEO)] & B3[(DB + replicas)]
    end
    GR[Geo router / gateway] --> BLR & MUM
    BLR <-.->|scatter-gather only at<br/>shard boundaries| MUM

Two compounding wins:

  • Latency — requests terminate in-region instead of crossing the country; matching data is local by construction.
  • Blast radius — a bad deploy or Redis incident in one region degrades one city, not the platform.

The only cross-shard case is a proximity search near a boundary (rider standing at the edge of two regions), handled by scatter-gathering the adjacent shards and merging. Keep boundaries in low-density areas and this stays rare. Use consistent hashing / careful boundary design for rebalancing as cities grow, and watch for hot shards — "Bengaluru" may itself need splitting long before "all of Karnataka" does.

One pattern worth noticing across all six deep dives: the ephemerality of the data kept licensing simpler designs. Locations refresh every 5s → Redis without durability guarantees is fine. Locks live 10s → single-instance Redis locking is fine. The only data that demands real durability — the ride itself and the money attached to it — lives in the primary DB and the Kafka log. Matching data lifetime to storage guarantees, instead of demanding maximum durability uniformly, is the quiet theme of the whole design.
